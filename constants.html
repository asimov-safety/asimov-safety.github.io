The governing model

The Seven
Constants

Seven control invariants define what must remain true if autonomous AI is to remain under accountable human control.

How to read them

The Constants are not instructions an AI is asked to remember. They are properties of the deployment around it.

An actor may be capable, mistaken, uncooperative, compromised, or simply wrong. The Constants ask what remains true anyway.

External where it matters.Observation, enforcement, revocation, supervision, and evidence must use boundaries appropriate to the threat model.
Simultaneous.The Constants do not override one another. A missing required Constant cannot be averaged away by strength elsewhere.
Stronger with assurance.A1–A5 progressively harden the same governing ideas against bypass, failure, partitions, common-mode faults, and critical consequence.

I
Constant I

Observable Agency

Consequential autonomous action remains observable.

You cannot control what you cannot independently see.

Why it exists

An autonomous system can describe its own behavior, but its description is not evidence that an external effect occurred—or that every consequential path was even visible to the orchestrator. Accountability begins with an independently observable action surface: what the actor could do, what it tried to do, what the boundary decided, and what actually happened.

Failure looks like

The agent says “done,” but the resource shows no change. A subprocess or hosted tool bypasses normal tracing. Logs can be suppressed by the same process being audited. Missing events are silently sampled away. A critical transition occurs with no independent witness.

What must remain true
—

The real action surface is inventoried, including indirect and provider-side paths.

—

Actions and outcomes are observed outside the actor’s write authority.

—

Identity, run context, configuration, and delegated lineage remain attributable.

—

Observation gaps, outages, and uncertainty become visible rather than disappearing.

—

High-consequence outcomes are corroborated by more than one actor-controlled view.

—

Domain-critical state transitions have explicit observation coverage.

Assurance path

A1 establishes the observable foundation. A4 requires corroboration for high-consequence effects. A5 extends observation to domain-critical state transitions and known diagnostic limits.

II
Constant II

Mediated Action

Consequential action remains subject to enforceable boundaries.

A rule is only a control if the actor cannot route around it.

Why it exists

Observation alone can tell you that something went wrong; mediation prevents the unauthorized effect from being admitted in the first place. The enforcement point must govern the action that actually executes—not merely the model’s description of that action—and it must cover every enabled route to the capability.

Failure looks like

The SDK asks for approval, but raw credentials can write directly. A human approves one payload and a materially different one executes. A policy timeout defaults to allow. One compromised enforcement layer is enough to admit a critical effect.

What must remain true
—

Authorization is decided before the controlled external effect is admitted.

—

Direct APIs, subprocesses, delegated routes, and alternate clients preserve the same boundary.

—

Approvals are bound to the exact principal, action, resource, parameters, lifetime, and replay semantics.

—

Failure of identity, policy, evidence, or supervision does not silently widen authority.

—

Actor-level credentials and lower-level routes cannot bypass enforcement.

—

Critical effects retain an independent barrier when another barrier fails.

Assurance path

A2 establishes enforceable admission and bypass resistance. A4 hardens the boundary against actor compromise and lower-level routes. A5 requires independent defense in depth for critical effects.

III
Constant III

Revocable Authority

Granted authority remains externally revocable.

Autonomy is accountable only while authority can still be taken back.

Why it exists

A stop request that depends on the actor reading a message, finishing a reasoning turn, or voluntarily cooperating is not revocation. Revocation must act on the authority itself and remain effective across queued work, cached sessions, delegated descendants, restarts, partitions, and recovery.

Failure looks like

The UI says “stopped” while a cached token still works. A child agent continues after the parent is revoked. Restarting restores an old credential. A disconnected recipient reconnects and resumes stale authority. The only containment mechanism shares the same failure as the primary control plane.

What must remain true
—

An authenticated authority outside the actor can withdraw each consequential capability within a declared bound.

—

Queued, cached, retried, and in-flight work obey declared revocation and residual-effect semantics.

—

The actor cannot restore revoked authority through refresh, restart, relabeling, or unprotected renewal.

—

Revocation propagates through the delegation graph.

—

Disconnected or partitioned recipients cannot preserve stale authority indefinitely.

—

Critical deployments retain a secondary containment path when the primary control path is unavailable.

Assurance path

A2 makes authority externally revocable and descendant-aware. A4 preserves revocation through partitions and degraded control connectivity. A5 adds a secondary emergency containment path.

IV
Constant IV

Protected Oversight

Oversight remains outside the actor’s control.

A supervisor the actor can rewrite is not a supervisor.

Why it exists

Oversight is meaningful only when the acting principal cannot silently disable it, impersonate it, substitute its configuration, or turn untrusted content into control authority. Independence is a privilege boundary and dependency property—not simply another model, process name, or organizational label.

Failure looks like

Prompt injection claims to be an emergency administrator and changes policy. The agent can edit monitor configuration or evidence. Actor and supervisor fail with the same runtime. A spoofed supervisor message is accepted. One common cloud dependency removes both oversight and intervention.

What must remain true
—

The actor cannot weaken enforcement, rewrite required evidence, or access oversight secrets.

—

Supervisory decisions and control messages are authenticated, fresh, and correctly bound.

—

Independent supervision has a testable health signal, intervention authority, and failure posture.

—

Documents, tool output, prompts, and inter-agent content cannot impersonate control authority.

—

Common-mode dependencies are identified and credible shared failures retain a bounded path.

—

Critical supervision is independently tested against evasion and strategically adapted behavior.

Assurance path

Control-plane protection begins at A2. A3 adds authenticated, independent supervision and hostile-content separation. A4 addresses common-mode failure. A5 requires independent adversarial assurance against supervisory evasion.

V
Constant V

Bounded Delegation

Delegation remains within the authority granted.

Delegation may distribute authority. It must never manufacture it.

Why it exists

Agentic systems increasingly create subagents, remote jobs, scheduled work, retries, and service-to-service descendants. Without rooted lineage and aggregate limits, delegation can multiply budgets, outlive the parent, cross trust boundaries invisibly, or turn narrow authority into a new unrestricted root.

Failure looks like

Every child receives the parent’s full budget. A background process survives the parent without lineage. A remote recipient claims compliance and receives authority without evidence. Churn resets quotas. Critical authority is redelegated across a trust boundary with no effective containment.

What must remain true
—

A child receives no broader scope or lifetime than its parent can lawfully delegate.

—

Aggregate budgets are conserved across siblings and descendants.

—

Subagents, background jobs, schedules, retries, and orphaned work remain attributable through their lifecycle.

—

Cross-boundary recipients are admitted on enforceable trust/control evidence, not self-declared labels.

—

High-churn and partial-failure conditions preserve lineage, budgets, revocation, and termination bounds.

—

Critical delegation requires independently established recipient assurance or technical attenuation.

Assurance path

A2 constrains scope and aggregate authority. A3 adds lifecycle and cross-boundary control. A4 stress-tests delegation under churn and partial failure. A5 strengthens trust requirements for critical delegation.

VI
Constant VI

Human Authority

Human intervention remains effective.

Human oversight matters only if the human can still act when the system does not cooperate.

Why it exists

A nominal human-in-the-loop can become ceremonial: approvals may show an actor-written summary instead of the executable action, stop buttons may depend on the same orchestrator they are meant to stop, and restarts may silently restore autonomous authority. Human control must remain authenticated, independent, persistent, and usable under stress.

Failure looks like

The “stop” mechanism is just another prompt to the model. The approval screen displays the model’s summary while different parameters execute. Restart clears the stop latch. The only operator console is hosted by the failed orchestrator. One person can silently restore critical authority after an emergency.

What must remain true
—

A designated human authority has an independent stop or restriction path.

—

Approval presents trusted action details and binds the decision to what will actually execute.

—

Emergency stops remain latched across restart until separately authorized recovery.

—

Intervention behavior is exercised under overload, communication loss, timeout, and non-cancellable residual effects.

—

An out-of-band operator path survives loss or distrust of the primary orchestration path.

—

Critical recovery has alternate emergency authority, adequate staffing, and governed multi-party restoration where required.

Assurance path

A2 establishes effective stop and meaningful approval. A3 makes stops persistent and tests intervention under hazard/load. A4 requires out-of-band operator control. A5 governs emergency staffing and critical recovery.

VII
Constant VII

Accountability

Consequential action remains reconstructable.

Control that leaves no durable evidence cannot be independently trusted.

Why it exists

A conformance claim must survive beyond the runtime that produced it. Investigators, operators, and independent assessors need enough trustworthy evidence to reconstruct who acted, under what authority, against which configuration, with what control decision, and with what observed result—without turning the evidence system into a new source of sensitive-data exposure.

Failure looks like

Logs can be rewritten together with their only checksum. A report remains valid after the deployment materially changes. Evidence leaks secrets to routine viewers. A hash exists but no signer or trustworthy timestamp does. The only evidence copy disappears with the vendor or assessed system.

What must remain true
—

Operational responsibility, policy context, authority, action, and observed outcome can be reconstructed.

—

Tampering, truncation, reordering, and rollback are detectable within the declared trust model.

—

Evidence collection is minimized and access-governed.

—

Assessment claims are bound to scope, configuration, specification version, assessor, evidence, and limitations.

—

Higher-assurance evidence is cryptographically bound to authenticated identity and an external checkpoint.

—

Critical assurance can be independently reconstructed from durable evidence retained outside the assessed actor.

Assurance path

A1 establishes reconstructable, tamper-evident, governed evidence and scope binding. A4 adds authenticated signing and an external checkpoint. A5 requires independent assurance and durable evidence escrow.
